chapter iv - mizuiro memory

2025-11-22

“Some people aren’t choosing death. They’re choosing an end to suffering that has replaced their life.”

“I cannot accept that, I don’t think anyone should be allowed to just… opt out. I don’t care how bad it gets. You don’t let someone die. You hold them. You stay. You don’t let them disappear. You keep them here until the hurt passes.”

For a moment they sat in parallel silence, both of them clearly hesitant to go first,  both of them typing and deleting, typing and deleting, in near to perfect unison. Though the coincidence brought a faint smile to his face, the synchronicity did little to mitigate the sharp edge of her exasperation towards him.

“It’s not about wanting to disappear,” she continued. “It’s about wanting to stop hurting in a way no one else can see.” 

“Yeah and if you're depressed and standing on the edge of something irreversible,” he typed back, “im most certainly not letting you go. I don’t care how dark it gets, i won't give up on you. Not ever.”

He casually scrolls up the conversation to reference one of her essays, written back in middle school when she was barely fourteen, not realizing that she isn’t concerned with or even cares about his valiant response. She just wants him to pull his head out of the self-dug well of idiocracy and actually comprehend the point she’s trying to get across.

“Yes, because helping the ill and giving them what they want is unethical. Apparently leaving them to suffer with their chronic mental illness or terminal disease, having their family see them suffer an excruciating death is unethical. When someone suffers so much to a point where they have to kill themselves painfully and with chance of fail, which may I add puts way more emotional pain to the family, that’s unethical.” It read.

He blinked. “Did you seriously write this when you were FOURTEEN?” The intensity in her writing, the way her cynical views were already flirting with her mind at the age of 14, pretty much left him stunned.

“Yes, i was mentally ill,” she replied nonchalantly.

“And you still believe in it now?”

“Not entirely, but yes.”

“To be fair… this is very well written for a girl of that age” he said with a smile on his face, before copying the entire paragraph to a clipboard, which he could refer to later, to tease her every now and then.

But the complexities of these emotions were far different than what he understood then, far beyond his comprehension of what a fourteen year old had written. His perception of depression was so wildly contrasted with hers that he couldn’t see the gulf until he was standing inside it. It was clear that they both viewed depression differently.

You see, before her departure, he never understood that not everyone wanted or could be “fixed,” that some forms of hurt were just something people had to live with. His conviction was naive, this belief that love and persistence could stitch a person back together. What he didn’t see or understand then, was how deeply personal suffering could be. How depression had its own logic, made its own rules, rules that certainly don’t welcome optimism. In time, he learned that sometimes broken wasn’t a state at all but a way of being, and insisting otherwise only pulled people further back into the dark.

And in retrospect, her beliefs were straightforward: that some people are too far gone, and that death might be the only exit they feel entitled to choose, if they ever had to make that choice. 

“Why can’t you just let her go?” His friend asked, long after they had their first conversation all those years ago.

“She’s got more inside of her than any girl i’ve ever met”. he replied. 

The weight of years threaded through his voice was quite obvious. Even the most coveted memories when revisited too often, begin to weigh on the heart instead of lifting it, and the very act of trying to preserve them perfectly can erode that same spirit that once embraced them.

He remembered every little detail, every little moment he had spent with her, almost as exactly as they had happened all those years ago. His passion for her had not dulled in the slightest; his enthusiasm for her; not a wee bit less than it had been all these years. And even though he carried this indelible tendency to piss her off, he remained certain that he possessed the strongest ability to make her laugh, better than anyone else could.

“I dream of her so much y'know? Every night when i go to sleep, i see her, sometimes five or six nights a week. In my dreams she’s happy, in some she’s sad, and i hate waking up from them, because the dreams are the only place where i can see her. And waking up means losing her all over again.”

“I just want the universe to be gentler toward her, kinder to her”.

He kept on insisting, as if he wanted to fight back against the universe itself, his words full of optimism and full of hope, just like he did valiantly all those years ago. He understood depression very clearly now, understood it as if he were wearing the very shoes she once talked to him about, the same shoes from which she had drawn her argument. He knew he wasn’t capable of being saved, that there was darkness all around him, no matter where he looked.

“And when she’s not in your dreams, what do you see?”

“i see mountains, far away lands, or maybe oceans sometimes.” 

“tell me, what do you really see?” His friend asked again, softer this time.

“i see darkness. an empty void. nothingness.”
